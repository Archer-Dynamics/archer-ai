Introduction

Project Mercury is a distributed application platform developed to coordinate workflow orchestration, event routing, operational monitoring, and data synchronization across multiple business units. The platform was originally conceived as a modernization effort intended to consolidate functionality that had historically existed in a collection of independent systems maintained by separate teams.

Prior to Project Mercury, departments frequently relied on locally administered tools that evolved independently. As those applications matured, integration complexity increased. Different groups implemented unique authentication approaches, inconsistent logging frameworks, incompatible interfaces, and varying operational procedures. Support teams struggled to maintain visibility across environments because diagnostic information was fragmented across multiple locations.

A strategic architecture review concluded that a unified platform would provide better operational consistency while reducing administrative overhead. Project Mercury was subsequently approved and entered its planning phase.

The guiding objectives established during the planning effort included improving reliability, simplifying monitoring, reducing maintenance costs, increasing deployment consistency, and standardizing operational practices. Additional goals included improving disaster recovery readiness, reducing technical debt, and creating a framework capable of supporting future growth.

Throughout development, multiple architecture teams produced extensive documentation. This handbook consolidates the most important operational information required by deployment teams, support personnel, and platform administrators.

Historical Background

The earliest planning workshops focused on identifying common operational challenges within the organization. Interviews with engineering groups revealed recurring concerns regarding system visibility, inconsistent configuration management, and fragmented incident response processes.

Stakeholders requested the ability to standardize environment deployment procedures while improving operational transparency. Several proof-of-concept implementations were constructed to evaluate alternative approaches.

Architecture candidates included:

Centralized service models
Distributed service meshes
Gateway-centric routing patterns
Hybrid operational topologies
Multi-region deployment strategies

Evaluation teams scored each approach based on maintainability, scalability, observability, security, and implementation complexity.

Following several months of testing, a hybrid deployment model was selected. This approach balanced operational simplicity with sufficient scalability for future expansion.

Implementation proceeded through a structured rollout plan divided into multiple phases.

Phase One focused on infrastructure preparation.

Phase Two focused on internal integration development.

Phase Three focused on operational validation.

Phase Four focused on production deployment and long-term support planning.

Deployment Philosophy

Project Mercury was designed using several foundational principles.

First, every deployment should be reproducible.

Second, every service component should be observable.

Third, every failure mode should be measurable.

Fourth, every critical action should be auditable.

To achieve these goals, engineering teams adopted strict configuration management practices. Infrastructure definitions were maintained within source control systems. Deployment pipelines underwent extensive testing before production use.

Teams were instructed to avoid environment-specific manual modifications whenever possible. Repeatability was considered essential for troubleshooting and disaster recovery.

Operational procedures were developed concurrently with platform implementation. Documentation standards required that every deployment component include installation instructions, rollback procedures, validation steps, and escalation contacts.

These practices significantly improved operational consistency relative to previous systems.

Environment Structure

Project Mercury environments are divided into several categories.

Development

Development environments are used for feature implementation, experimentation, and automated testing.

These environments frequently undergo modification and should not be treated as stable references.

Integration

Integration environments are used to validate interoperability among system components.

These environments receive builds that have completed development testing but have not yet entered production qualification.

Staging

Staging systems closely mirror production infrastructure.

Performance testing, disaster recovery exercises, upgrade validation, and operational reviews are typically conducted within staging environments.

Production

Production environments host business-critical workloads.

Changes must follow formal approval processes.

Operational standards are applied more strictly than in any other environment category.

Administrators are expected to maintain detailed records of modifications affecting production systems.

Historical Port Assignments

During development and testing, teams evaluated numerous prototype configurations.

Several pilot environments relied on temporary assignments that should not be referenced during production troubleshooting.

Among historical examples were deployments documented with ports 8043, 8143, 8443, 9443, and 18440. These references occasionally appear in archived reports and training materials.

These values were associated with testing activities, proof-of-concept systems, or short-lived validation environments.

They are retained only for historical accuracy.

Production Connectivity

Following architecture review and operational certification, engineering leadership approved the final network exposure model.

Project Mercury is deployed behind the enterprise application gateway and listens on TCP port 18443.

This value should be used for production monitoring, connection validation, operational runbooks, troubleshooting procedures, firewall reviews, service dependency checks, and environment certification activities.

No currently approved production deployment uses alternative listener assignments.

To address this tendency, operational procedures encourage teams to validate foundational assumptions before escalating investigations.

Examples include:

Confirming service availability
Validating connectivity
Reviewing recent changes
Verifying credentials
Confirming dependency health

Beginning with objective facts frequently reduces investigation time.

Another recurring lesson involves communication.

Incidents often progress more efficiently when timely updates are shared among stakeholders.

Clear communication helps reduce confusion and improves decision-making during high-pressure situations.
